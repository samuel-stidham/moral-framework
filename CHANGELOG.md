# Changelog

All notable changes to this framework are documented in this file.

This project follows semantic versioning (MAJOR.MINOR.PATCH). See `CLAUDE.md` for the full versioning rules.

## [3.0.0] - 2026-08-29

Rebuilt the harm policy's institutional rule as version 3.0.0, a MAJOR revision of the policy. Rule V3 previously made procedural regularity sufficient, so an institution following its own lawful procedures could impose any harm. Two earlier designs failed adversarial review and were discarded. The first bounded institutional authority by terms the adopting framework supplied, which left the check inside the party being checked. The second imported the whole Declaration, which no institution satisfies, so every arrest became resistible. Harm imposed by an institution now violates no duty only when it is lawful, uses fair process, and stays within a floor. The floor states nine bars on acts, in the policy's own words. Each names the institution, so no bar reaches private conduct. The discrimination bar keeps every ground the Declaration lists, and the word arbitrarily carries the relevance test. The belief bar covers practice and the receiving of information, and excludes threats, deception, and calls to violence. The slavery bar excepts work the law requires of everyone alike, which the Declaration does not, and the Lineage says so. They draw on Universal Declaration of Human Rights Articles 2, 4, 5, 6, 7, 9, 11, 18, 19, and 25. Each bar judges a single act, never the institution as a whole. No institution's own law moves the floor, and no permission in the policy reaches harm it bars. The custody bar is the sole exception, since care validly refused is not withheld. The Two Tracks index rule no longer reaches Rule V3, whose every term is judged from outside the institution. Fair process means the institution's own lawful procedures. Before punishment that deprives liberty it also means a public hearing before an impartial tribunal. It means notice, a real answer, and the presumption of innocence. A competent person may waive the hearing. Those come from Articles 10 and 11. The Definitions now define person, anchored to every human being, so the term left the delegation list. Fair process left it too, and five terms remain. Rule V1 bars an institution from putting a person to death as punishment, and no consent makes a punishment something else. Other institutional killing outside a live danger needs valid consent. The Declaration is silent on capital punishment, and the Lineage says so plainly. The Danger definition keys its institutional exclusion to the act, not the institution. Institutional harm is answerable with force only where a responder perceives a breach of the floor. Failing the other conditions may breach the duty and licenses nothing. Harm the killing bar forbids stays qualifying whatever a responder perceives. Judgment and Law now states that the policy guides the reader's conduct and judges institutional acts without commanding institutions. It adds that the policy does not decide what one nation owes another, and licenses no one to seek out a danger. It no longer bars private force outright, since the license track turns on a live danger. The Permitted harm definition states that unpermitted harm is no violation by itself. The Brief and the Purpose were rewritten to match. Principle 1 now states support for the Declaration in full, all thirty articles, without reservation. It also notes that the policy's floor is narrower. The Preamble's first step gains the harm boundary as a constraint on its balancing. The principles introduction names the boundary as the floor under all four. Principle 3 carries the reasoning for the killing bar. It also drops the last Non-Aggression era wording and restates the floor in the policy's terms. `docs/RELEASING.md` now states how a policy revision maps to the framework version, and that only the framework version is tagged. The 2.4.1 entry below now records that it shipped inside the v2.5.0 release. Four rounds of adversarial review, twelve reviewers in total, shaped the result. Added the United Nations (1948) reference to the policy, cited in the Person definition, Rule V3, and the Lineage. Corrected APA italics on the Skelton, Frowe and Parry, and Sander-Staudt entries. Split one over-length sentence in the consent definition. The principles introduction now places the harm boundary above all four principles, and the Preamble's first step runs it before any weighing. Restructuring the principle hierarchy makes this a MAJOR revision of the framework.

## [2.5.0] - 2026-08-11

Rebuilt the harm policy from scratch as version 2.0.0, a MAJOR revision of the policy. Five adversarial reviews of version 1.1.1 converged on thirteen defect clusters. The rebuild was developed in three staged drafts, each gated by a fresh adversarial reviewer before the next stage began. The policy now runs two declared tracks, one for violations and one for the response license. New definitions cover intent, careless risk, consent, threat, danger, source, and torture. The license carries four conditions, with necessity and proportionality separated and torture barred absolutely, in act and in threat. Consent is bearer-sovereign, with guardian and emergency clauses, and the floor takes Feinberg's anti-paternalist line. A bystander clause permits minor property harm in grave escapes, with repair owed. Added references: Feinberg (1986), Thomson (1991), and Frowe and Parry (2021). Principle 3's adoption sentence now names danger and the licensed response. Three external review rounds tightened threats, staging, repair allocation, resets, mitigation, torture's reach, and the guardian presumption before release. A plain-language summary and a judgment-and-law clause close the policy as a personal decision procedure. A good-faith rule makes deliberate exploitation of the text itself a violation. A final round made the brief the controlling spirit, deepened the law clause, and added an amendment rule. A last Gemini round yielded three targeted fixes on legal validity, consensual torture, and innocent-source repair. The law clause now carries a kinetic exception, so no statute removes a licensed response to grave live danger. The amendment rule's contradiction trigger no longer requires an ordinary case. This is a MINOR revision of the framework.

## [2.4.1] - 2026-08-11

Clarified the harm policy's threat definition and bumped the policy to 1.1.1. This entry carries no tag of its own. It shipped inside the v2.5.0 release. A threat is now plainly a display of serious intent through words, conduct, or both. The old wording said expression and listener, which skewed toward speech alone. The initiation definition already declared that the medium does not matter, so this aligns the two. This is a PATCH revision.

## [2.4.0] - 2026-08-11

Revised the harm policy to version 1.1.0. The definitions now include intent and careless risk. The threat definition follows the true threat doctrine, so hyperbole and jokes do not qualify. Initiation now requires intent, covers any medium, and includes fraud and engineered provocation. Defense keys on reasonable perception of a threat rather than the attacker's hidden state of mind. A new Dependencies section names the terms the adopting framework must define. Added two legal references, Watts v. United States and Counterman v. Colorado. The framework adopts the policy in whole, so the policy revision expands Principle 3. This is a MINOR revision.

## [2.3.0] - 2026-08-11

Added a standalone policy document, `policies/harm.md`, stating the framework's harm rule in full. The policy opens with definitions of harm, threat, initiation, and consent. Its rules bar initiating harm and careless risk, then set three conditions for justified defense. The defensive license ends when the threat ends and never transfers to grievance or group. Principle 3 now adopts the policy by reference. The policy carries its own References section, citing Feinberg, McMahan, and Skelton. It is written in a neutral voice so others can copy it without adopting the framework. Also removed a stale version number from the principles introduction, which still read 2.1.0. The document heading remains the single home for the current version. This is a MINOR revision.

## [2.2.0] - 2026-08-11

Replaced the Non-Aggression Principle in Principle 3 with nonmaleficence, the duty not to harm or injure others. The old label drew its line only at force and fraud, and carried a political lineage the framework had to disclaim. Nonmaleficence covers every kind of harm and matches the principle's own words, a refusal to inflict harm. Added the Skelton reference for W. D. Ross's prima facie duties. Removed the Van der Vossen reference with the NAP passage it supported. This is a MINOR revision.

## [2.1.0] - 2026-06-26

Moved the Van der Vossen citation to the Non-Aggression Principle sentence it supports. This is a MINOR revision.
