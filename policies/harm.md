# The Harm Boundary - Version 3.0.0

This policy defines what I mean when I commit to avoiding harm. It gives that commitment a clear boundary and a consistent application.

In this framework, harm is the violation of a person's human rights. I believe every person holds these rights simply because they are human. Governments can recognize and protect them, but their existence does not depend on government approval.

I use the Universal Declaration of Human Rights as my primary account of these rights (United Nations, 1948). It gives the clearest and most complete description that I know.

The structure behind this policy comes from the Declaration of Independence (United States, 1776). It describes people as holding unalienable rights. It describes government as an institution created to secure them. I take that basic structure from the 1776 text. I take the fuller account of human rights from the Universal Declaration.

National law serves a separate purpose in this framework. It turns the boundary into rules that people can apply and courts can enforce, defining specific offenses, procedures, remedies, and penalties. The boundary itself begins with human rights.

## The Role of Law

National law is where human rights usually become concrete and enforceable. When a law protects a recognized right, I treat it as a practical expression of the boundary.

The Universal Declaration also recognizes that the exercise of a right may sometimes require limits (United Nations, 1948, art. 29). Those limits must protect other people's rights, or serve the just needs of a democratic society. A restriction that exceeds those purposes crosses the harm boundary.

This distinction matters because law and morality can separate. A law may permit something that violates human rights. Such a law shows what the government allows, but it does not tell me that the action is right. I apply this standard to every country, including my own.

## The Role of the State

The same harm boundary applies to the state. I cannot violate your rights and claim that violation as a right of my own. The Universal Declaration gives no person a right to destroy another person's rights (United Nations, 1948, art. 30). When the state prevents such a violation, it takes no right from me. It stops me from crossing a boundary that already applied to me.

The government must still justify its own use of force, and Locke helps me explain where I believe that authority comes from. In Locke's state of nature, each person holds the authority to judge and punish violations of natural law. Political society transfers that private authority from the individual to the community (Locke, 1690, secs. 128-131). I understand this exchange as a surrender of private enforcement. The underlying rights remain with the people.

Government therefore acts with delegated authority. It must follow a law that existed before the conduct occurred. It must state the charge and give the accused a fair hearing (United Nations General Assembly, 1966, arts. 14-15). Any restriction it imposes must be tied to a specific offense and must last no longer than that offense supports. A restriction that outlives the sentence that justified it has become a punishment nobody imposed.

Any punishment must also remain within national law and the human-rights boundary. A valid law tells me that the state followed its own rules. I still have to ask whether those rules respect human rights. When the state crosses either limit, it causes the same kind of harm it exists to prevent. Public authority does not change the nature of the violation.

The International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights makes some of these protections especially clear. It holds them in force even during a public emergency. They include freedom from torture and slavery, and protection against retroactive punishment. They cover recognition before the law, and freedom of thought, conscience, and religion. They also cover protection against arbitrary deprivation of life (United Nations General Assembly, 1966, arts. 4, 6, 7, 8[1]-[2], 15, 16, 18). A criminal conviction does not cancel these protections. An emergency cannot suspend them. They do not depend on a majority's permission.

These provisions matter because state power often expands during a crisis. A crisis may change which restrictions are necessary, but it cannot make a person less human.

Punishment follows the same principle, and it can limit a person's liberty without erasing their rights or dignity. A man serving thirty years remains a person with human rights. The state must treat every prisoner with humanity and respect their inherent dignity (United Nations General Assembly, 1966, art. 10). A sentence gives the government specific authority for a defined purpose and period. It gives the government no authority beyond those terms.

When the sentence ends, the authority to confine ends with it.
